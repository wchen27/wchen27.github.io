01

six cameras capture an instant in time

tool orange · C++, GigE Vision · Johnson Lab, moments-behavior

the rig is an aluminium extrusion frame with six high speed cameras bolted to it, running over fiberoptic into network cards in the workstation. 3d printed and plexiglass arena in the middle, strobes on capacitor banks synced up with the cameras on a PTP clock. i designed the rig from the ground up, and the layout moved a lot before it settled.

the cameras are strobe locked, so frame 5000 on one of them is the same moment as frame 5000 on the other five. everything after this assumes that's true, and triangulation has no way to notice when it isn't. it just quietly hands you a 3D point that's a bit off, on every single frame. i designed a resync method that reads the PTP timestamps off each camera and re-indexes all six onto one timeline, so you can check for dropped frames and fix them before they become a problem.

the other thing is the encoding. the pictures coming off the camera are from a massive 65 megapixel full frame sensor, and the rig is running at 300 fps. there's too much information coming off the camera for the encoders to keep up, and sometimes they silently drop frames. i implemented a per camera drop counter that gets written into the recording, so you can check for dropped frames after the fact and resync if necessary.

  • in an animal, an arena, a protocol
  • out 6 synced streams @ 300 fps
  • presets full frame, cropped ROI
  • guard per camera drop counters and canonical timeline resync
02

undoing the glass

tool calibrate · OpenCV, charuco · built into red, moments-behavior

i designed this whole pipeline around one failure. an older two stage calibration hit 1.245 px reprojection error, the best number the rig had ever produced, but reconstructed a machined post target 35% too small.

that comes straight out of how the objective is set up. bundle adjustment treats every board corner as a free 3D point, and nothing in that setup knows how big the board actually is. focal length and scene depth can trade off against each other along a direction where the image residuals never move. on that run focal drifted from about 11,200 px to 21,250 px and reprojection improved the whole way.

two things fall out of that. scale has to be pinned by an object you measured with calipers, and the test verifies calibration quality has to come from outside the calibration. reprojection stays a diagnostic here. it gets reported at every stage and trusted at none of them.

then there's the glass. a flat window shifts every ray sideways, and for the 2 mm acrylic in the arena that's about a millimeter, which lands as 10 px or more on the sensor. the shift runs along each camera's own line of sight, so no single 3D point reprojects correctly in all six views at once. you can't subtract it afterwards. you have to calibrate through the glass, on the same optical path you're going to record on.

so i split it into five recordings and gave the chain one rule. each stage locks everything the stage before it established and fits only what's new. the sections below are what each recording is for.

01 charuco_open → stage A · air model

focal length, on a clean path

the only recording where the plexiglass comes out. big board, widest field i can get, nothing in the light path. it's solving for focal length, the camera positions and the world frame, and these are the best conditions it'll ever get to do that in. the distortion it fits gets thrown away, since it was measured with no glass in the way. the sanity check for this stage is implied pixel pitch. divide fx by the lens and you should land on 3.12 µm pixels. if you don't, the number is wrong no matter how prettily it reprojects.

02 charuco_full_glass → stage B · glass model

what the glass actually does

panes back in, small board on a rod through the door. focal and extrinsics are locked from the last stage, so everything in this recording gets spent on the handful of parameters that describe the glass. the interesting one is tangential distortion. every other pipeline in red freezes it. here it's free, because a flat window pushes rays in a direction, and p1 and p2 are the only part of the distortion model that has one.

03 charuco_cropped_glass → stage B · same solve

same optical system, smaller window

this one folds into the same solve as the last. same lens, same focus, same pane, just a different piece of the sensor, so the cropped corners get lifted back onto full sensor coordinates and pooled with the last recording into one dataset. two things shift together and neither is optional. corner positions move by the ROI offset, and frame indices have to move past the full frame ones, because landmark ids are built from them and letting them collide would fuse two unrelated board poses into a single 3D point.

04 posts_full → stage D · metric gate

the stage that doesn't fit anything

printed posts on the arena floor, tips clicked by hand. this stage only measures. it triangulates the tips using the calibration you've built so far and compares them against the layout the posts were printed from. the comparison runs on sorted pairwise distances, so it doesn't care about world frame, handedness, or where you started clicking, and it ranks candidates on shape error, which is scale free, so it still finds the right correspondence when the scale is wrong.

05 posts_cropped → stage E · crop transform

checking the crop is where it says it is

same posts, cropped preset, nothing touched in between. moving to the ROI is a pure pixel translation, so cx and cy shift by the offset and everything else stays exactly where it was. that transform gets measured directly, and that's the whole point of the stage. a wrong ROI offset looks identical to a principal point shift, so anything free to move cx and cy would quietly absorb the error this stage exists to find and then report a beautiful zero. your full frame clicks get shifted into the crop and drawn on top. a label sitting off a post tip means either the offset is wrong or the click was.

‹ swipe ›

  • stage A fits focal, extrinsics, world frame
  • stage B fits cx, cy, k1, k2, p1, p2
  • stage D fits shape, scale
  • pass scale within 2%, shape within 2%
  • good run scale 1.007 · shape 0.18%

finding

letting the tangential terms move was the change that did the most. lock p1 and p2 and the glass shift has nowhere to go, so it gets absorbed into cx, cy and k1 instead.

03

creating the datasets

tool red · C++, triangulation · Johnson Lab, moments-behavior

ground truth is a person clicking on joints and no software gets around that. all you can change is how efficient your clicks are. red shows all six views at once, and once you've placed a keypoint in two of them, triangulation puts it in the other four. a frame that would've taken twelve clicks takes two or three.

pump dispenses are indexed against the video, so you can jump straight between reward events instead of scrubbing around hunting for them. frames go green once every keypoint has enough views to triangulate, so whether you're done with a frame is just something you can see.

  • in recording + calibration + skeleton
  • views to place 2 of 6
  • skeletons Bee10, or your own JSON
  • out a JARVIS training dataset

05

fighting storage and desync

tool cluster_pose harness · LSF, rolling buffer, desync gate · Johnson Lab and collaborators

two things get in the way of running this at any kind of scale.

first, the numbers just don't fit. about 40 TB of raw video, 3.7 TB free on the store that processes it. so the driver keeps a rolling buffer, stages a few recordings at a time, and deletes each one the moment its prediction comes back.

the second shaped the design more. triangulation takes the same frame number from all the cameras and calls it one instant. if a camera drops a frame partway through, everything after that is off by one on that camera, and the 3D you get is wrong in the worst possible way. it never crashes, and it never looks obviously wrong. so nothing gets submitted until it clears a desync gate. it reads the PTP timestamps off each camera, works out the frame interval from the timestamps themselves instead of trusting whatever fps was configured, and re-indexes all camera videos onto one timeline. on a clean recording the plan comes out all zeros and the output is byte identical to skipping the gate entirely. that's how i checked it wasn't changing things on its own.

the second clip below is a fly recovering from having a front leg removed. five legs, a gait nothing in the training set really covers, and the model still tracks it cleanly. that's the case i most wanted to see it handle, since the whole point of the injury sessions is that the animals don't move normally.

fly data Whole-body 3D kinematics of freely behaving Drosophila Ispizua, Abe, Yan, et al. · bioRxiv 2026.05.03.722293, 2026

  • recordings 165 across 4 sessions
  • raw video ~40 TB into 3.7 TB free
  • per recording 8 way GPU split + dependent merge
  • gate clean · trim · reindex

design note

the harness targets L4s on purpose. the pipeline is bottlenecked on CPU video decode, so a wide pile of cheap L4s clears more recordings per hour than a handful of A100s, and they schedule sooner. H100 nodes are out of scope entirely, since the pinned environment needs torch 1.10.1+cu113.

06

from coordinates to behavior

tool green · C++, Dear ImGui · Johnson Lab and collaborators

3D coordinates still aren't a result. that's where the green visualizer comes in. green loads the whole prediction database, runs a classifier over it to find behaviors, and then hands you everything you need to check its work. arena path maps, a 3D pose player, per trial detail, and a histogram of the population that redraws while you drag the filters around.

rejections get broken out by reason, so "too few walking cycles" and "too short" are just numbers sitting on screen. the filter is the last thing between raw pose and a claim in a paper, and it shouldn't be the part nobody can look into.

the demo above is running on flies, and that's the one place in the whole chain that's animal specific. everything upstream already handles both. orange, the calibration, red and cluster_pose only ever see cameras, a skeleton definition and a calibration, so pointing them at a bee is a config change.

green is close to the same. the classifier reads 3D keypoint trajectories out of the prediction database, and that database has the same shape no matter which rig filled it. what actually changes is the skeleton it indexes into and what counts as a bout. for the flies a bout is a walking cycle, found off tarsus tip trajectories against the body axis. for bees the same machinery points at a Bee10 skeleton and asks a different question, antenna pointing or time spent at the reward port. the arena maps, the 3D player, the trial table, the population histogram and the CSV export never learn which animal they're looking at.

bees also get something the fly sessions don't have. pumpctl writes every dispense into the recording timeline, so there's an actual event to align behavior against.

  • in 40 recordings of predicted 3D pose
  • filter 6,842 candidates, 1,313 accepted
  • rejected 110 too few cycles · 27 too short
  • out 5,811 cycles · 794.3 s of walking
  • to port swap the skeleton, redefine the bout

either end of the pipeline

the two programs making studying interesting behaviors possible

pumpctl

reward hardware

runs the Poseidon syringe pumps on firmware i rewrote for this rig. manual, jog and experiment modes. you set a dose in µL and a delivery time, and you can save a whole experiment as a program of dispenses and waits. syringe bore matters a lot here, since the calibration was done against one specific inner diameter, so it lives in the config. swap syringes, update the number, doses stay right. every dispense gets timestamped into the recording, and that's what red's jump to reward reads.

beemover

archival

copies recordings off the local drives onto the network storage. six cameras at 300 fps fill a local disk quickly enough that moving data becomes something you do between trials. least clever program here, and the one i ran the most.

a different rig entirely

separate frame, separate cameras, separate build

the galvo cam, and how it keeps a telephoto lens on a moving bee

everything above is one rig. this is another one, built alongside it to do something the six camera setup can't.

a 135 mm lens gets you the detail you want and a field of view nowhere near big enough to hold a walking bee. normally you'd just give up the magnification. here the camera stays put and the view moves instead. three cameras run a YOLO detector, the centers of their boxes triangulate into one 3D point, and two pan tilt mirrors bend a fourth camera's line of sight onto that point. the galvo cam gets a close up anywhere in the arena.

the geometry got modelled first to check the camera placement would give usable triangulation across the whole working volume and not just near the middle.

calibrating it is about learning how mirror angles map onto positions in the arena, so a target gets swept through the volume while the mirrors chase it.

  • detector cams 3, running YOLO
  • target triangulated bbox centroid
  • steering 2 axis pan tilt mirrors
  • result 135 mm lens, full arena coverage
